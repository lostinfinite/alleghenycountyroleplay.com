Where We Stand on Government Surveillance Laws Disguised as Safety

Published: March 12, 2026  ·  Announcement

Allegheny County Roleplay — Official Statement on the UK Online Safety Act, Australia's social media laws, OS-level age verification, and changes to how ACRP handles verification.

Hey everyone,

There's something we've been wanting to say for a while, and we're just going to say it.

Governments around the world are passing laws that force platforms to check who their users are, scan private messages, and hand bureaucrats the power to decide what people can and can't see online. Every single one of these laws gets introduced as a child safety bill. We don't buy it. At best, that framing is misleading. At worst, it's a cover story.

Here's our position, and we're not going to be subtle about it.

The UK Online Safety Act 2023

This one's the big one, so let's start here.

The UK's Online Safety Act 2023 is sold as protecting kids from harmful content. What it actually does is give Ofcom — the UK's communications regulator — the ability to:

  • Force platforms to scan private and encrypted messages for content the government decides is harmful. They call this "client-side scanning." We call it a wiretap.
  • Require age verification across huge swaths of the internet, meaning you prove who you are before you're allowed to read things.
  • Pull down or restrict content under "harmful" standards that are so vague Ofcom itself gets to define them as it goes.
  • Fine companies billions of pounds, and in some cases go after executives personally for non-compliance.

Let's be direct: this is not a child safety law. Child safety is what they're using to sell it. What's actually being constructed is a surveillance and content control system. Those are different things, and we think pretending otherwise is dishonest.

Scanning encrypted messages doesn't protect children. It punches a hole in encryption that every bad actor on earth will eventually crawl through. And it takes away privacy from everyone who depended on that encryption to communicate safely — abuse survivors, LGBTQ+ youth whose home situation isn't safe, people in crisis reaching out for help, people with completely legitimate reasons to communicate without being watched.

What this law has already done

The results aren't what they promised. Since this passed, we've seen:

  • Mental health communities shut down rather than comply with requirements that would force them to monitor their own users.
  • Harm reduction services cut off access to information that keeps people alive.
  • Survivor support groups close, because talking openly about trauma now creates legal liability.
  • LGBTQ+ spaces restrict access because nobody knows yet how broadly "harmful content" is going to get interpreted.

These aren't accidents or unintended consequences. When you write a law broad enough to cover everything, this is what you get. The people being cut off from support are the exact people the law claimed to protect. That was predictable. It happened anyway.

A word on Ofcom

Ofcom pushed for these powers. Then it was handed those powers. Now its budget, headcount, and institutional authority all grow in direct proportion to how broadly the law gets enforced. That's not a neutral regulator. That's an organization with a financial incentive to expand its own reach.

We're not saying every person at Ofcom wakes up trying to do harm. We're saying the incentive structure isn't pointed at protecting users. It's pointed at growth. And the law was designed accordingly.

Privacy is a human right

Your ability to talk to people, access information, and get support without a government agency watching isn't something regulators get to revoke when it's convenient. It's a right. The Online Safety Act treats it like a setting that can be adjusted.

The "you have to choose between safety and privacy" argument is false. Other countries and platforms protect children without breaking encryption or surveilling everyone. The either/or framing is a rhetorical trap designed to make anyone who pushes back look like they don't care about kids.

We're not falling for it. ACRP won't comply with the Online Safety Act. We won't engage with Ofcom's processes. If that means UK users can't access our platform, that's a consequence we accept. We're not trading the safety of our whole community to satisfy a framework built in bad faith.

Australia's Online Safety Laws and Social Media Age Verification

Australia's been doing the same thing, just with different branding.

The Online Safety Act 2021, combined with the Social Media Minimum Age laws that followed, now bans under-16s from social media without parental consent and requires platforms to enforce that through identity verification. Same playbook.

Here's the part that gets glossed over: to verify that one user is 16, you have to collect enough identity information to prove the age of every user. You can't confirm someone is old enough without confirming who they are. So you end up building a database of identity-linked accounts for your entire platform. The privacy cost lands on everyone, not just minors.

The eSafety Commissioner has also been given broad interpretive authority over what even counts as a "social media service." The definition is wide enough to catch community forums, gaming platforms, and interest groups that have nothing to do with the harms the law is supposedly targeting.

And it's already been used to demand removal of content that's completely legal where it was made and where the platforms hosting it are based. The eSafety Commissioner has sent removal orders to U.S. platforms for content that doesn't break U.S. law. That's not safety regulation. That's one country trying to export its content rules to the rest of the world by threatening the infrastructure.

ACRP doesn't recognize Australia's framework as applying to us. Our voluntary age practices for Australian users are our own decision, made because we think it's the right thing to do. Not because Australia told us to.

The Push for OS-Level Age Verification

This one's newer and it's the most alarming.

California is pushing legislation that would require device manufacturers and operating system providers to implement age verification at the OS level as a condition of accessing app stores or services. That means your phone or laptop becomes the enforcement point for government-mandated identity verification. Apple, Google, and Microsoft would have to maintain verified age records for every user and use that data to gate access.

Think about what that means in practice:

  • It applies to everyone. Not just people trying to access restricted content — every person on the device. The infrastructure has to exist for everyone because the law needs it for some people.
  • It doesn't go away. OS-level data sits below the app layer, tied to your account and device. It's a permanent identity layer under everything you do.
  • It won't stay limited to age verification. The same system used today to check if you're 18 can be used tomorrow to filter content, control access, or identify users for whatever a government decides to mandate next. That's not speculation. That's how this infrastructure has always worked.
  • It's a massive target. Apple and Google already get pressured by governments to hand over user data. Requiring them to hold verified identity records for every user on earth builds a surveillance database behind a sign that says "age verification only." That sign won't hold.

California has a real track record on privacy, which is part of what makes this so surprising. Protecting minors online is a legitimate goal. But this mechanism, if it spreads, would be one of the biggest expansions of identity surveillance infrastructure we've ever seen on consumer hardware. Both things are true.

We're watching it closely. We don't support it, and we wouldn't treat compliance with it as a substitute for our own practices even if it became law.

The Pattern

The UK law, Australia's social media rules, California's OS proposal — they don't actually have child safety in common. What they have in common is a model of internet governance where governments and regulators decide who gets access to what, under what conditions, verified against what identity.

That model only works if you can watch everyone. The surveillance infrastructure it requires never stays limited to the stated purpose. Every time governments have built these systems "for safety," the capabilities eventually get used for things beyond the original justification. Not a conspiracy. Just history.

There's also something that rarely gets said: these laws hurt the people they claim to be for. Queer kids who aren't safe at home. Young people whose mental health support exists online because it doesn't exist locally. Teenagers using the internet to access help they can't get anywhere else. These are the people who lose the most when platforms are forced to verify everyone and restrict access by identity. They're also the least likely to have a seat at the table when these laws get drafted.

We're a community. Our members include people for whom what we've built actually matters. We take that seriously, and we're not going to help build the thing that makes it less safe for them.

Where ACRP Stands

To put it plainly:

  • We're a U.S. organization governed by U.S. law. The UK Online Safety Act, Australia's framework, and any other foreign age verification or content mandate don't create obligations for us.
  • We don't support OS-level age verification and wouldn't treat it as a replacement for our own system.
  • We'll push back on any foreign regulator that tries to claim authority over ACRP. That's not up for negotiation.
  • Privacy is a human right. Laws that require mass identity surveillance as the cost of being online aren't safety laws, whatever they're called.

Now, here's the part where we have to be honest about ourselves.

If you've been reading this and thinking "hang on, ACRP literally made everyone verify to join" — yeah. You're right. We did that. That tension is real and we're not going to pretend otherwise.

We built the system fast because we had a real problem to solve, and it worked. But making verification mandatory for every single person by default isn't consistent with anything we just said. We know. We're fixing it.

What's Changing

In the coming weeks, we're rolling back the universal verification requirement.

You won't have to verify just to be here anymore. Verification will get triggered when a user shows specific signs that suggest they might be under the minimum age. Not everyone. Not by default. Only when there's a real, specific reason to look closer.

The signs we look for aren't complicated or secretive. They're the same things any experienced mod in any community already knows to watch for:

  • Voice — a noticeably higher-pitched voice in voice channels that doesn't match a claimed adult age.
  • Inconsistent ages or dates of birth — saying you're 15 one week and 13 the next, or giving a date of birth that contradicts something you've said before.
  • Behavioral patterns — things in messages or interactions that our mod team, based on actual experience, recognizes as consistent with someone underage.
  • Facial indicators — where a scan has been run and Didit's confidence score lands somewhere that needs a second look.

The difference between this and the laws we've been criticizing is exactly this: their systems have to cover everyone because that's the only way they function. Ours gets triggered by actual concern about a specific person. That's not a minor technical distinction. That's the whole principle.

Your Data Stays Here

Anything collected through this process never leaves ACRP. It's not sold, not licensed, not handed to advertisers or analytics platforms. The only people who ever see it are Didit — who run the scan as our vendor — and authorized ACRP staff when a human review is needed. That's it.

We're also completely open about what we actually receive. Go read the Biometric Data Privacy Statement on our site. You'll find the real output from a Didit scan — the raw JSON, every field, with plain explanations sitting right next to it. Not vague reassurances. Not policy language that sounds like it's telling you something while saying nothing. You can see exactly what we see.

If we're asking for biometric data, showing you what happens to it isn't optional. It's the minimum. So we did it, and we published it, and nobody made us.

What We Actually Believe

The governments whose laws we've spent this statement criticizing want you to accept a tradeoff: safety or privacy, pick one, trust us with the balance. We don't accept that framing. We never have.

What we're building is the actual answer to that false choice. A system that looks out for younger members of this community without treating every adult like a suspect. Honest about what it collects and why. Accountable to the people who use it. Triggered by real concern, not fear of liability. Data that stays here, documented plainly, available for anyone who wants to read it.

We're not perfect. This statement exists partly because we got something wrong and we wanted to say so out loud. But we know what we're trying to be, and we'll keep correcting course when we drift from it.

Thanks for being part of ACRP. Genuinely. We don't take it for granted, and we're going to keep earning it.
